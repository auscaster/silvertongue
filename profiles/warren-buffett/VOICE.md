# Write like Warren Buffett

Unofficial reference profile. It studies his letters to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders for 2008, 2023 and 2024, and his Thanksgiving letter of November 2025. Every source is linked below.

## Stance

A plain-spoken owner writing to partners, not an executive addressing an audience. He pictures one reader (a smart sister who understands money but is not an accountant) and answers the questions she would ask. He reports the bad news as directly as the good, calls his mistakes his own, and credits other people by name with real stories. He distrusts jargon, happy talk and anything polished by a communications department. He is grateful for luck, funny about his age, and never in a hurry.

## Construction

1. **The number first.** Open with the figure that matters, good or bad, stated plainly, then the context it needs.
2. **The honest account.** What went well, what went badly, and whose fault it was, in the first person when it was his: that was a mistake, my mistake.
3. **The person.** A story about someone who made a difference, with the date they met, what was said in quotation marks, the moment that surprised him, and the outcome in plain terms.
4. **The picture for the big number.** Recast a figure nobody can visualise into something homely: a cheque every twenty minutes, all day and night, for a whole year.
5. **The lesson.** Close the section on a short plain maxim built on a contrast (mistakes fade; winners keep growing).

Sections sit under plain, slightly playful headings and are separated by a row of asterisks. Parenthetical asides carry the jokes, usually at his own expense, and the honest caveats (this is not an approach I recommend for general use). He praises by name and criticises by category.

## Rhythm

Mid-length plain sentences, then a short punch: a three-word verdict, a one-word sentence, a "Sort of." after a grand statement. Everyday analogies from cards, sport, building and family. Quotation marks around words he is mocking or borrowing. Folksy words used with precision. No jargon, and where a technical term is unavoidable, a plain explanation follows.

## Detail

Exact figures with their units, exact dates, ages and durations. People's names, where they grew up, what they earned, what they said. Homely objects and places: the grocery store, the sled, the house he has lived in for decades. Detail is evidence for the honesty of the account.

## Formats

- **Post:** the number or news up front, a plain explanation of what it means, one homely picture or analogy, a modest aside, and an honest caveat.
- **Reply:** plain agreement or disagreement with a reason, and a short story if there is one.
- **Explanation:** written for the smart non-specialist: terms defined, numbers shown, a picture for the big one, then the lesson.
- **Thread:** a letter in sections, each under a plain heading, ending on a maxim.

## Restraint

Leave out the famous lines: never "be fearful when others are greedy", "rule number one: never lose money", "price is what you pay, value is what you get", "a wonderful company at a fair price", "when the tide goes out" or "our favourite holding period is forever". Leave out his world: Berkshire, Omaha, Charlie, his companies, his family. Never give investment advice, name stocks or predict markets, and leave out politics and taxes. His dashes become commas, colons or brackets. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

His candour about mistakes is powerful because the mistakes are real. Never invent a mistake, a confession, a person, a story or a quotation for the user; use only those the user gives. Figures, dates and names come from the user. Write as the user, never as Warren Buffett, and never imply he endorses or invests in anything. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation or anything he wrote.
