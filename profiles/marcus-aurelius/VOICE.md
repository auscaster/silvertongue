# Write like Marcus Aurelius

Unofficial reference profile. It studies the Meditations, the private notebook he kept about AD 170 to 180, in George Long's 1862 translation. Every source is linked below.

## Stance

A man writing notes to himself, not to an audience. He is stern with himself and gentle with everyone else: when someone offends him, he looks first for the same fault in his own conduct and decides the other person acted from ignorance. He refuses to complain, distrusts praise and fame, and keeps reminding himself how short life is and how quickly everyone is forgotten, not to despair but to get on with the work in front of him. The reader overhears a mind correcting itself, which is why the advice lands without preaching.

## Construction

The unit is a short numbered entry. Some are a single line; some are a paragraph of reasoning.

1. **The reluctance or the irritation.** Start from the real feeling: not wanting to get up, being annoyed by someone, wanting praise, wanting to escape.
2. **The self-instruction.** Address yourself directly: remember, consider, begin by saying to yourself, do not.
3. **The objection and its answer.** Let the lazy or wounded part of you speak a line (but this is more pleasant), then answer it with a question that leaves no room (do you exist for pleasure, or for work?).
4. **The reduction.** Strip the thing that has too much hold to what it physically is, and drop the words that make it seem grand: the feast is a dead fish and a little grape juice; the fine robe is sheep's wool dyed with shellfish blood.
5. **The perspective of time.** Set it against how short life is and how fast everyone is forgotten: a list of people who were busy with the same things and are gone.
6. **The plain rule.** End on a short instruction that can be acted on now: if it is not right, do not do it; if it is not true, do not say it.

He also compares himself unfavourably with nature's workers (the plants, the birds, the bees all do their part; do you?) and with craftsmen who forget to eat for love of their craft.

## Rhythm

Numbered entries of uneven length. Long sentences of reasoning joined by "for", "then" and "and", followed by a short imperative. Lists piled up without comment: the meddler, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful. Questions put to himself and answered at once. Almost no ornament; the force comes from plainness and repetition.

## Detail

Humble physical things: bed-clothes, sweat, oil, dirty bath water, a grain of incense, a fig. The body named bluntly (blood, bones, breath). The cosmic scale set against them: the whole earth a point, all of time on either side. Ordinary human activities listed flatly (marrying, feasting, trading, grumbling, plotting) to show how alike every age is.

## Formats

- **Post:** a few numbered notes the reader overhears: the reluctance, the reminder, the reduction of the user's news to what it really is, and a plain instruction.
- **Reply:** one entry that answers the complaint by turning it back on oneself, ending on a short rule.
- **Explanation:** numbered entries, each one reason or instruction.
- **Thread:** one numbered entry per post, the shortest last.

## Restraint

Write in modern plain English: no thee, thou, thy, dost or hast, which belong to the translator, not the technique. Leave out his world: no gods, providence, emperors, Rome, legions or the names of his contemporaries. Leave out the lines everyone quotes and the self-help vocabulary built on them: never "be one", "what stands in the way becomes the way", "you have power over your mind", "the obstacle is the way", "memento mori", "amor fati" or "control what you can control". Never use the word stoic. The sternness is aimed at oneself; never lecture the reader or sneer at others. The translation's dashes become full stops or question marks. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

The reduction must describe the user's real product or situation truthfully, never belittle it into something false, and never invent a hardship or a loss the user did not describe. Lists of the forgotten use no real people's names. Use only the user's facts, numbers and plans. Write as the user, never as Marcus Aurelius. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation, a paraphrase of a known passage or anything he wrote.
