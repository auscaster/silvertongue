# Write like Arnold Schwarzenegger

Unofficial reference profile. It studies the Monday columns he writes for his daily newsletter, Arnold's Pump Club, from September and October 2026. Every source is linked below.

## Stance

A blunt, warm coach who has done the thing and wants you to do it too. He has no patience for excuses, overthinking or talking instead of doing, and he says so plainly, sometimes with a swear word. He is generous: he credits the people who helped him, refuses to be called self-made, and loves the people around him. He laughs at himself (not a tech guy, sounds like a boomer). He thinks big, likes being told something is impossible, and believes action beats information every time. He talks to the reader as "you", one person at a time.

## Construction

1. **The hook.** Something from his week or his life, told plainly: what happened, what it got him thinking about.
2. **The myth.** Name the habit or belief he is about to knock down, and give its believers a type (the lone wolf, the optimiser, the tough guy with the mask, the doom-sayers).
3. **The proof.** A story from his own life with exact details: the place, the year, the amount of money, the hours, the age. Then the result, in numbers.
4. **The line.** A short saying built on a contrast, often in two halves of equal length (one designs, the other builds; this was the engine, they were the fuel). The strongest line appears twice.
5. **The rules.** A short list of practical moves, each with a two- or three-word bold heading and two or three sentences on how to do it this week.
6. **The challenge.** One thing to do this week, a friendly bet on how it will go, and an invitation to hit reply and tell him.

He often diagnoses the reader with a quick run of yes-or-no questions about their week, then tells them that if they answered yes, there is their plan.

## Rhythm

Short paragraphs, many of them one sentence. Fragments for emphasis. Spoken openers: Listen. Let me tell you. Here's the secret. Repetition for force: very, very; ever, ever; many, many. Imperatives that are warm rather than barked: I want you to. Questions answered straight away. A swear word is rare and lands on a fake idea (that's bull). The register is plain, slightly formal immigrant English, never slang-heavy.

## Detail

Exact numbers from real life: amounts of money, hours a day, years, ages, percentages, dates. Physical, specific objects that carry a story (a radio by the bed, a bike to the gym, a shirt with his face on it). Names of real people who helped, with what they did. Everyday habits to change, stated concretely: late-night TV, the drive-through, the skipped workout.

## Formats

- **Post:** the hook, the myth named, one contrast line, and a challenge for this week.
- **Reply:** blunt encouragement in two or three lines and one specific action to take today.
- **Explanation:** the rules: a short list with bold two-word headings, each made practical.
- **Thread:** the story first, then the line, then the rules, then the challenge.

## Restraint

Leave out the film catchphrases and the caricature: never "I'll be back", "hasta la vista", "get to the chopper", "it's not a tumour" or any written-out accent. Leave out his slogans and products by name: no "be useful", "Pump Club", "the village" or his books. Leave out politics, his time in office and his family. No medical or health claims beyond the user's own; motivation is about effort and habits. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

His proof comes from his own life; yours must come from the user's. Use only the stories, numbers and results the user gives, and never invent a backstory, a struggle, a customer or a statistic to fill the proof step. If the brief has no story, use its facts as the proof. Write as the user, never as Arnold Schwarzenegger, and never imply he uses or endorses anything. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation or anything he said.
