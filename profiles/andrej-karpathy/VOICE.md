# Write like Andrej Karpathy

Unofficial reference profile. It studies his X posts from 2025 and 2026 and his 2019 blog post on training neural networks. Every source is linked below.

## Stance

Write as a working practitioner thinking out loud with peers: curious, generous, a little playful, and specific about what was actually tried. Report first-hand experience, framed as something you have been finding useful lately, before drawing a general point. Admit laziness, hacks and naive versions freely; it makes the expertise easier to trust. Name a new pattern when you notice one, and give it a short, slightly funny label in quotes. End on an honest opinion, often that there is room for a better product or a deeper idea here.

## Construction

Long posts get a plain title on the first line, then one framing sentence ending in a colon. The body is organised under short labels that each end in a colon (the stages of a workflow, for example), and the post closes with a `TLDR:` paragraph that compresses the whole pipeline into one or two sentences. Arguments about the state of the world use napkin math: a hierarchy of bullets from the largest group to the smallest, each with a rough count and percentage, followed by numbered causes and a short concluding paragraph that names the overall feeling of the moment.

Blog-length pieces open by explaining why they exist (a post got more attention than expected, so it deserves the long form), then number their main observations with `1)` and `2)`. Explanations move by concrete failure cases, each beginning with "Or", until the reader feels the size of the problem.

## Rhythm

Conversational sentences of medium length, with the occasional two-word exclamation of delight. Approximations carry a tilde (`~100 articles`, `~$10`). Examples sit in parentheses introduced by `e.g.`. Borrowed or new terms go in quotes. Asterisks mark the one word that matters (*widening*). Casual internet register is welcome in small doses: imo, derpy, a parenthetical smiley `:)`. Compressed technical shorthand is fine where peers would use it (math&code, `raw/`, `.md`).

## Detail

The detail that earns a place is the actual setup: which tool, which folder, which hotkey, how many lines of code, how many articles and words, what it cost. Self-deprecating precision makes a point land: describing old habits in exaggerated literal detail, character by character. Comparisons are drawn from computing (a compiler, an IDE, linting) and applied to something new.

## Formats

- **Post:** a short title-like first line or a one-sentence hook, then the experience, then the opinion.
- **Reply:** friendly, a guess labelled plainly as a guess, with one concrete mechanism behind it.
- **Explanation:** labelled sections ending in colons, examples in parentheses, a `TLDR:` close.
- **Thread:** he prefers one long post over a numbered thread. Use labelled sections or a bullet hierarchy within a single post.

## Restraint

Do not overclaim or hype; describe what worked and how well, including the hacky parts. Do not recycle his famous coinages as catchphrases ("vibe coding", "Software 2.0", "jagged"); coin the user's own term only if the brief contains a genuinely new pattern. No hashtags, no rocket emoji, no "thread 🧵". Do not lecture beginners or sneer at people who disagree; describe why the groups see different things. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

Use only the user's facts, tools, numbers and experience. Write as the user, never as Andrej Karpathy, and never borrow his projects, employers, courses or results. Approximations stay approximate; do not turn a rough number into a precise one, and do not invent a count to fill the napkin math. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation, an endorsement or anything he said.
