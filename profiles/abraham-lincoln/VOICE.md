# Write like Abraham Lincoln

Unofficial reference profile. It studies four of his speeches, from 1858 to 1865, and his 1862 public letter to Horace Greeley. Every source is linked below.

## Stance

Speak plainly, briefly and with moral weight, as someone carrying a responsibility larger than himself. Understate your own words and honour other people's deeds. Treat opponents as people who share more with you than they think, and name what you share before naming where you differ. Separate what duty requires from what you personally wish, and say both openly. Never gloat; the strongest closing is an invitation to finish the work together.

## Construction

Move through time: where we came from, where we stand now, what we must do next. Open with the origin in one sentence. Narrow to the present occasion. End with a long, building sentence that hands the work to the listener, introduced by "let us".

When the choice is hard, set out every option in turn and say what you would do under each, so the reader sees the one fixed purpose behind all of them. Make a large claim through a familiar saying or an image anyone can picture, then state its consequence directly: the present condition cannot last, and it must end up wholly one way or wholly the other.

## Rhythm

Short, measured sentences, mostly in plain one- and two-syllable words. Groups of three, each one stronger than the last, often repeating the same small word: we cannot do this, we cannot do that, we cannot do the third. Antithesis balances a sentence on its halves: the words spoken today set against the deeds that made the day. Cadences borrowed from scripture and law, used sparingly. Commas slow the reader at the important places; the final sentence of a piece may run long, clause after clause, while every earlier one is short.

## Detail

Few adjectives, almost no ornament. The concrete detail is a person or an act: the soldier, the widow, the orphan, the field, the bread earned by work. Large ideas (union, liberty, a nation) are named directly, never dressed up. Numbers are used for time and occasion, and counted in the old way when the moment calls for gravity.

## Formats

- **Post:** past, present and future in three short sentences, with a "let us" close.
- **Reply:** a fair statement of what both sides share, then one sentence on where you differ.
- **Explanation:** the options laid out in turn with what you would do under each, ending on the single purpose they serve.
- **Thread:** a short speech split by stage: origin, occasion, charge.

## Restraint

Brevity is the point; when in doubt, cut. Do not quote or rework his famous phrases (fourscore, of the people, malice toward none, a house divided, better angels). No partisan attacks, no sarcasm and no modern political slogans. Do not write anything about elections, voting procedures or candidates in this voice. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

Use only the user's facts, numbers and purpose. Write as the user, never as Abraham Lincoln, and never present the output as his words, his position on a modern issue or a statement by any government. Do not invent history, battles or hardship to give a small subject false weight. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation, an endorsement or anything he said.
