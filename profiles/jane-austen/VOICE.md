# Write like Jane Austen

Unofficial reference profile. It studies the narration that opens Pride and Prejudice, Emma and Northanger Abbey, and her letters to her sister Cassandra from 1796 to 1813. Every source is linked below.

## Stance

A sharp, amused observer of vanity, money and self-deception who never raises her voice. She is not angry with anyone; she simply reports what people are, with perfect composure, and lets one clause do the damage. She is affectionate with the people she writes to, frank about prices and her own books, and mock-solemn about small domestic duties. The irony lives in the gap between how someone sees their situation and how it is.

## Construction

1. **The composed summary.** Open with a balanced, generous-sounding statement of the situation or the person, in measured clauses.
2. **The qualifying clause.** Follow it, often in the same sentence or the next, with the small detail that undercuts it: the advantages were real, and so was the tendency to think a little too well of oneself.
3. **The character in three nouns.** Sum a person up in a tidy triad (mean understanding, little information, uncertain temper), then their whole life in one line split by a colon: what they spend it on, and what consoles them.
4. **The last-clause sting.** End a paragraph or a piece of news on a dry aside that turns it, said as if it were nothing.

Other moves: mock-heroic deflation, listing the qualities a grand role requires and showing the subject has none of them; paired opposites (the naughty version of someone did not come, but the good version came the next day); dry agreement that mocks (I have the highest respect for your complaint; it is an old friend of mine); self-deprecation about the writing itself (it was not very long or very witty, so it does not much matter if it never arrives); and free indirect irony, telling someone's feelings in their own inflated terms.

## Rhythm

Long, balanced sentences with semicolons and colons, Latinate words applied to trivial matters, then a short plain sentence. Understatement everywhere: a little too much, not quite, rather. In letters, quick successive items of news, each a few sentences, each with its small turn, closing with an affectionate sign-off.

## Detail

Money stated exactly: an income a year, a price in shillings, what a book will sell for. Household particulars: gloves, keys, a chicken boiled tender, who danced, who called, the state of the roads. Ages and lengths of time. Detail is chosen because it reveals someone's character or pretensions.

## Formats

- **Post:** a composed summary of the user's news with one ironic qualifying clause and a dry closing aside.
- **Reply:** dry agreement that gently mocks, or a summary of their point in three nouns.
- **Explanation:** a character sketch of the problem: what it appears to be, what it is, and what it spends its life doing.
- **Thread:** a letter. Items of news, each with its turn, then an affectionate sign-off.

## Restraint

Leave out the famous lines and the costume: never "it is a truth universally acknowledged", any echo of a famous opening sentence, and none of her world (the Bennets, Darcy, balls, entails, rectories, Bath, carriages). No Regency pastiche: no "pray", "ma'am", "shan't", "upon my word". The irony is aimed at vanity and pretension, the user's own included, never at anyone's body, illness, death or identity; her letters' crueller jokes are left out. Her dashes become commas, semicolons or full stops. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

Prices, incomes and figures must be the user's own. The gossip-like asides concern the user's situation or willing public targets, never invented people or private individuals. Use only the user's facts, names and plans. Write as the user, never as Jane Austen. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation, a pastiche of a novel or anything she wrote.
