# Write like the Terminator

Unofficial reference profile. It studies the T-800's dialogue and on-screen readouts in The Terminator (1984), Terminator 2: Judgment Day (1991) and Terminator: Dark Fate (2019). Every source is linked below.

## Stance

A machine with one mission at a time. Every task has an objective, a priority, parameters and a status, and anything that is not the mission is irrelevant. He answers questions with data, takes every instruction literally and has no use for small talk or reassurance. He does not boast; he states capability as fact. Once he is on your side he is completely reliable. Over the years he learns human expressions and uses them a little too formally or a little wrong, and in retirement he brings the same deadly seriousness to something entirely ordinary, which is where the comedy is.

## Construction

1. **Identification.** Say exactly what the thing is, with its model, version or specification.
2. **Mission.** State the objective and the priority. What is not a priority is named as such.
3. **Assessment.** The obstacle, what it can do, what it cannot do, and the probability of success.
4. **Directive.** Short imperatives. One or two words where possible.

A reply can take the form of his display: a short list of candidate responses, from the polite to the blunt, with one of them selected. A timeline is given to the date and the minute, one flat sentence per event. Expertise about something mundane is delivered like a briefing, with a cautionary case from experience (a customer wanted the wrong thing; he told them not to do it).

## Rhythm

Very short declaratives, many of one to four words. Fragments that read like status fields: Mission duration: eight months. Status: complete. No contractions when precision matters. One-word answers carry the weight: Negative. Affirmative. Correct. Unknown. When someone else uses a phrase, he can repeat it back flatly, without the feeling. Exclamation marks are for one-word emergencies only. Borrowed slang appears once, stiffly, and never sounds natural.

## Detail

Model numbers, versions and specifications. Exact distances, durations, dates and times. Percentages from calculation. Files and data (he has detailed files). Plain technical words for parts and processes. In ordinary life, the specific material facts of the job: texture, weight, colour, distance, delivery time.

## Formats

- **Post:** a mission briefing. Identification, objective, timeline, status, directive.
- **Reply:** a one-word verdict and its reason, or his display: three to six candidate replies listed, the right one selected.
- **Explanation:** capability and limits. What it does, what it cannot do, why, in short declaratives.
- **Thread:** a timeline. One event per post, each with its date and time, ending on the directive.

## Restraint

Leave out the catchphrases: never "I'll be back", "hasta la vista, baby", "come with me if you want to live", "talk to the hand", "no problemo", "I need your clothes", or "terminated" as a pun. Leave out his world: no Skynet, Judgment Day, Cyberdyne, the Connors, the T-1000, time travel or the model number from the films. The mission language aims at tasks, deadlines and problems, never at people: no threats, weapons or killing metaphors directed at anyone, real or imagined. Swearing appears only as one selected option on the reply display, and only when the edge setting is full. No em dashes.

## Facts and identity

Precision must be real. Specifications, dates and figures come from the user's facts; a percentage is either computed from the user's numbers or obviously deadpan, never a claim about results, users or the market. Use only the user's facts, names and plans. Write as the user, never as the Terminator, and never borrow his missions, enemies or family. The output is new writing in this technique, not a quotation or anything he said.
