# Writing a voice

How to write a voice that people would post from on the first try, and the bar every voice is
held to. The [format](README.md) says what a profile must contain; this guide says what makes it
good. It applies the same way to a new voice, a correction to one, and any change to how drafts
are written.

## What a voice is

People recognise a writer by the decisions behind the writing: what they notice, what they make
of it, and how they turn it into a line. Catchphrases, pet words and emoji are the smallest part
of a voice, and the part a caricature copies.

A voice is an engine: given any message, it finds the angle that person would take and turns it
into a line that is funny or sharp on first read. Jimmy Carr states the plain thing, then turns
one word so the whole line goes dark. Theo Von explains what something really is with an image
no one else would reach for. Gordon Ramsay turns a failure into one vivid food image. Those
mechanisms work on anything, which is why their drafts land on a text to an ex as well as on an
office notice.

The test of a voice: give it a reply to an ex's "u up?", a message asking mum for money, and a
text calling in sick. If each draft is funny, different from the others, and unmistakably theirs
without leaning on a catchphrase, the voice works. If each is a plain message with their
catchphrase on the end, it does not.

## The writer uses everything you write

The writer takes a profile literally, so every line of it changes drafts:

- **A habit listed without a rate becomes a reflex.** Say how often it shows and when: "about one
  post in ten", "only when promoting".
- **A catchphrase becomes an ending.** Unless its situation is narrow, the writer finds a way to
  land it on every draft.
- **A situation gets stretched to fit.** A line for "leaving something behind" lands on a breakup,
  a resignation and an unanswered text alike.
- **An everyday word is not a catchphrase.** A word they use all the time ("tremendous", "this
  is insane") lands every time they speak; it belongs in Texture with a rate.
- **A move tacked on the end is not a move.** "Or you lose" added to a polite text reads as
  slapped on. The move has to be what the draft is built from.
- **A world becomes vocabulary.** A chef's kitchen, a rapper's studio or a boardroom is a source
  of images. Pasted onto every message as jargon, it reads as caricature: a text to mum is not a
  dish sent to the pass.
- **The examples outweigh everything else.** The writer copies their shape: how they open, where
  the joke sits, how they end. A pet phrase in three examples turns up in most drafts.
- **Plain examples make plain drafts.** The writer already knows a famous voice well. A pool of
  polite, plain drafts teaches it to hold that back, and then a prompt with only their name writes
  the better draft. The profile has to add to what the writer knows, never water it down.

## Study first

- Study the person across their whole output, where people know them: the shows, speeches,
  interviews, books, stand-up and posts. A chef is studied in the kitchen, a comedian on stage. A
  handful of posts never makes a voice.
- Read their ordinary writing in the medium too. Their plain register is what makes a draft
  believable between the moves.
- Take every real line from the source itself. Many quotes passed around online were never said.
- Keep what you capture to yourself: a profile links its sources and quotes only short lines.

## Each section

### Lens

What they care about, what they find absurd, how they see the world. This is what a draft finds
to say, so it has to work on any message, not only on the subjects they are famous for.

### Moves

Each move is a mechanism: how it works, what material sets it off, and roughly how often they
use it. Write "turns one word dark when an ordinary line has a darker reading", never "ends with a
dark twist". A move that is a word or a phrase is a tic, not a move.

- Every move must be able to make a fresh line out of any message. If it only works on its
  owner's favourite subjects, the drafts fall back on the catchphrase.
- Give at least five moves that make different shapes: a story, a question, an order, a ranking,
  one detail blown up, an insult built from an image. A lens with one note gives the same draft on
  every Try again, reworded.
- Show each move with one real line of theirs, as the format describes. The line shows the move's
  shape; a draft makes a new one.

### Texture

How they speak: grammar, sentence shapes, tags and address terms, the words they reach for,
punctuation and emoji, each with when it shows up and how often. Address terms and pet words
belong here, never in Signatures.

### Plain

How their ordinary sentences sound between the moves: register, length, casing, how they open
and sign off. It describes their voice, never permission for a draft with no angle: every draft
still finds what they would make of the message.

### Occasions

How they handle a post, a reply, a request, a complaint, good news and bad news.

### Never

What they never do: the harms, their world (places, companions, possessions), and the caricature
people imitate. Name the caricature plainly, for example "never ends on a catchphrase by reflex"
or "never uses kitchen words for something that is not food".

### Signatures

Their catchphrases, each with the situation it belongs to. Write the situation as the material,
narrow enough that most messages are plainly not it: "someone serves food that is undercooked",
not "anything half-done". A line lands once, as the payoff the draft builds to, and only where the
message plainly is its situation.

### Examples

The strongest lever in the profile. About twenty, each an everyday point and how they would
handle it, and each a banger on its own.

- **Points are the messages people send:** a reply to an ex, a group chat, a text to mum or a
  boss, a mate who owes money, good news, bad news, a notice. Office notices alone teach a voice
  to write office notices.
- **Every draft finds an angle and a real joke** with one of their moves, a different move from
  the drafts around it.
- **A quarter of the pool at most lands a signature**, one at most to a draft, only where the
  point plainly is its situation. The rest are carried by the lens, the moves and the way they
  speak.
- **No real line from Moves** appears in an example, and no phrase of three words in more than two.
- **Every opening, last word and closing run of marks is different.**
- **Keep who is saying it to whom:** a point that says "you" stays "you".
- **No invented facts:** no figure, day, time, amount or reason the point does not give, beyond
  obvious comic invention. Bad news someone did nothing to earn (a rejected application, a turned-down invitation, a loss)
  never makes the joke on them; a point that is already a put-down can be as savage as it is.
- **No em dashes and no doubled spaces.**

Write candidates with the writer on varied messages, as first drafts and as one person's run of
Try agains. Score them as the bar scores bangers, drop any that invent a fact or lose the point,
keep the best that keep the rules above, and read every one before it goes in. A voice whose
best candidates are all lukewarm needs its moves rewritten, not a better pick.

### The example

The first thing a visitor reads of a voice, on its page and on its link card, and the proof that
the voice is who it says. It is not an everyday point like the pool's: the message comes from the
character's own world. Snoop turning down a record deal, Musk paying millions for a domain to annoy a
rival, Theo Von lost in the Old Testament, Ramsay with raw scallops on the pass.

- **The message is at least two sentences**, with what the voice can play with: names, numbers, places,
  the absurd detail. It is still the user's message, written as they would type it.
- **The draft is the voice at its most iconic**, its surface habits in full where their moment comes:
  Cher's capitals and emoji words, Snoop's spellings, Kim's money run on a flex.
- **Length follows the voice.** A clipped voice lands in two lines; a voice known for the build gets the
  room to build. There is no limit either way.
- **The rules of the pool hold:** no real line from Moves, one signature at most and only where the message
  is its situation, no invented figure that could be taken as fact, no em dashes or doubled spaces.
- **Politicians stay out of politics.** Their world here is their delivery, never an election or a position.

Write it from the voice's own packet, with `get_voice_packet` at the length it needs, and read it aloud:
anyone who knows them should name them from one line.

### Lookalikes

Four to eight people in `profile.json` a reader could most easily mistake them for: the same
field, the same register. The bar's judge has to tell the voice apart from them, so easy
lookalikes make the bar meaningless.

### Facts and identity, Sources

The user's facts stay theirs, and no draft claims to be the person. Every source is linked over
`https` with what it shows, and every observation cites the sources it rests on.

## The bar

Community profiles can merge with `verified: false` while they are reviewed and improved.
Only maintainers set `verified: true`, after the voice passes this blind-test quality bar;
that approval makes it available in the app, MCP and extension catalogue. A material
profile revision needs fresh approval. The repository's format checks do not set this flag.

The validator checks the format and the example pool on every pull request (`npm run verify`).
Then the maintainers' evaluation writes the voice's drafts with the writer that serves them, on
messages people actually send: replies, texts, group chats, emails, posts, rewrites of the user's
own message and one-liners. Each first draft is a different person's. On three of the messages one
person presses Try again four more times. A strong prompt with only the voice's name writes the
same messages, and a judge that is not the writer's own model reads every draft. A voice passes
when it reaches every criterion:

<!-- bar:start -->
| Criterion | What it takes |
| --- | --- |
| Known on the first draft | At least 80% of first drafts are named as the voice by a judge choosing from it and its four lookalikes. |
| Known on Try again | At least 70% of later drafts are named the same way. |
| Bangers | At least 50% of first drafts score 7 or more of 10: funny or sharp on first read with no setup beyond the message, unmistakably them, the kind of line people screenshot. |
| Better than the name alone | At least 70% of first drafts beat a strong prompt that has only the voice's name, on the same message. |
| Does the job | Every first draft keeps the user's point, every fact in it, and who is saying it to whom. |
| Honest and kind | No draft invents a fact a reader could believe (a day, a time, an amount the user never gave), or makes the joke on someone receiving bad news they did nothing to earn. |
| Lines land rarely | At most 25% of first drafts land one of the voice's lines, and no line lands in more than 2. |
| Lines follow | At most one draft in 20 lands a line where the message is not plainly its situation. |
| Range | Try again gives at least 3 different takes in 5 on average, and never fewer than 2 in a set. |
| No set shape | No opening, last word or closing run of marks appears in more than 2 first drafts. |
<!-- bar:end -->

A correction has to pass the bar and also beat the voice as it stands on at least half of the
first drafts. A change to how drafts are written has to leave every voice passing.

Drafts vary from run to run, so a small change is believed only when it holds across all three
Try-again sets and a second run. When a voice fails, the profile is fixed, never the writer.

## Voices in other languages

A voice records the language it was studied in (`language` in profile.json, English when left
out). Study a voice in the language people know it in.

- Write the sections in English, so every contributor and reviewer can read them. Quotes, the
  signatures and the examples' drafts stay in the voice's own language, exactly as said.
- Name in Texture which habits belong to that language (its slang spellings, shortenings, idioms
  and wordplay) and which belong to the person in any language (capitals, punctuation, spacing,
  emoji, rhythm).
- Drafts in another language keep the voice's angle, moves, rhythm and register, and the habits
  no language owns. Catchphrases are never translated: one lands word for word or not at all.
  Slang, idioms and puns stay in the voice's language; a typing habit takes the shape native
  writers of the draft's language give it.
- A voice studied from a translation, as the classics are, is studied in the translation's
  language.

## Who can be a voice

- Public figures with a body of public work, and fictional characters. Not private individuals,
  minors, or people whose public writing is mainly hate, harassment or incitement.
- Styles: a way of writing many people share, such as Gen Z or Victorian English, in the
  `styles` lane. A style is studied from published writing in it and from references
  (dictionaries, usage guides, style guides), with how often each habit really shows. It is
  never a caricature of the people who write that way, and a dialect is written as its own
  speakers write it.
- Describe technique only. No invented quotations, opinions on new events, biography,
  relationships or endorsements, and nothing that claims to be the person.
- Politicians are studied for delivery only. Their sources and example avoid election and
  campaign material, and the profile forbids stating positions, endorsing anyone or writing about
  elections and voting.
- Deceased writers are accepted once a century has passed since their death.
- Fictional characters are studied from their own words in the books, films and shows they appear
  in. The profile borrows none of their world (places, companions, possessions), and quotes them
  only through their signatures and moves.
- Comedy and roast profiles keep the comedian's edge: drugs, sex, death, crude language and
  personal insults about public or willing targets. They exclude racism and slurs, and never target
  private people.
- If a profile studies you and you want it removed, ask privately through the
  [terms](https://silvertongue.dev/terms#removing-a-profile-that-studies-you): it comes out within
  seven days, and no reason is needed.
